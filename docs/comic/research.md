# Webcomic research

Done 2026-10-09, after the first proof of concept (beat A of book 1, chapter 1), at the
owner's request: "take a step back and do some research on the web." The owner's own
reading of the proof: the characters look static, which follows from reusing one pose and
one zoom level; details like the counter look off.

**How much to trust it.** Almost all of this is craft advice from working creators, tutorial
sites and blogs. None of it is data about what keeps readers: no source gave retention
numbers, and the search found none. Where a source was a vendor's marketing page (several
AI comic-generator sites turned up) it was not relied on. Numbers for gutters and fonts
disagree between sources and are starting points to try on a phone, not rules. Wally Wood's
panel sheet was found by name and history only; its drawings were not seen.

## What the proof of concept does wrong, against the research

Each line is a finding below applied to `ch01.png`, as I read it.

| the proof | the research says |
|---|---|
| Every panel shows one or two full-length figures about 340 px tall, centred, at the same distance | Vary the shot: wide to set the place, medium for talk, close-up for emotion, extreme close-up for a detail |
| Characters stand in a row facing the viewer, not each other | Staging and eyelines carry a conversation; give speakers something to do |
| The same 20 px gap between all panels | Spacing is the pacing lever in a vertical scroll: small for speed, large for a pause, very large for a scene change |
| A long caption in A1 | Convert interior thought into something visible; keep captions few |
| A5, the beat the whole beat is about, is one panel | Break an action into anticipation, action and outcome; more panels slows it |
| Flat boards with bold seams; a counter that is a box | Backgrounds should support the mood and sit back in value; depth in three zones |
| Nothing the face cannot say is drawn | Manga symbols (sweat drop, anger vein, gloom lines) carry what a simple face cannot |

## Findings

### 1. Shot variety is the first cure for "talking heads"

- The head-and-shoulders shot is the easy default and overusing it makes pages repetitive.
  Remedies named: change the angle and the composition, frame through something in the
  scene (a window, a reflection), pull back to the environment or an object, and give
  speakers "business" to do while they talk (the listener stirring a cup, a cut to the cup).
- Shot sizes and what each does, from one comics glossary: extreme wide (the place, the mood),
  long wide (character in the setting), full (posture and action more than emotion), medium
  (waist up, the common one), medium close-up (face and body language), close-up (reaction),
  choker and extreme close-up (a single detail, subtle feeling).
- Test of whether the talk is the problem: some creators argue talking heads are only a
  problem when nothing else is happening, and that the question is whether the dialogue is
  interesting. Wally Wood's "22 panels that always work" is the well-known toolbox for exactly
  this, assembled by Larry Hama from Wood's sketches.
- A critique heard repeatedly: every figure at the same size, in the same part of the frame,
  doing the same thing. Vary size, placement and posture.

### 2. In a vertical scroll the gap is the pacing

- A working webtoon creator: "the larger the distance, the more readers have to scroll," so
  the gap sets the tempo. A wide gap is a pause; narrow gaps are speed. A long gap implies time
  passing or a change of scene.
- Numbers, which disagree: 50 to 100 px between ordinary panels in one guide; a minimum of
  about 200 px per panel and 600 to 1000 px for a scene or location change in another (both
  for an 800 px canvas). Treat as a range to try on a phone.
- Platform: WEBTOON takes 800 px wide, up to 1280 px tall per image, 2 MB each; Tapas takes
  up to 940 by 4000. Not checked against the platforms' own current rules.
- Limits that keep a phone screen readable: at most two panels visible at once; no more than
  three bubbles per panel; no more than four sentences per bubble; large type, 12 to 16 points
  on an 800 px canvas, larger than print. Long horizontal panels force the phone to rotate:
  avoid them.
- An action is anticipation, action, outcome in three panels; "the more panels you add in
  between the action, the slower it'll feel."

### 3. The transitions between panels (McCloud's categories)

Moment-to-moment (slow, many near-identical beats), action-to-action (one subject, the common
one), subject-to-subject (a cut to another subject in one scene), scene-to-scene (leaps in time
and place), aspect-to-aspect (several views of one moment, to set a place or a mood), and a
rarely used non-sequitur. One critic argues the categories blur and closure is the better lens.
Used here as vocabulary for planning a sequence, not as law.

### 4. Lettering has conventions, and they are checkable

- Western reading order inside a panel: the first balloon highest and furthest left, later ones
  down and to the right; plan the flow across the whole strip, not panel by panel.
- The tail points at the speaker's head or mouth, and an open mouth marks who is speaking.
  Tails must not cross each other, nor cut across another character's face or neck. A
  consistent tail length helps.
- Balloons go over empty background, not over faces or key action. An outline that touches a
  face, a border or another balloon makes a tangent that confuses the eye.
- Several creators place the text first and draw round it, so there is room.
- Fonts: comic lettering fonts are chosen to read fast at small sizes in tight balloons; the
  best-known foundry is Blambot (priced fonts, licences to check). Our bundled Gelasio is a
  serif, which reads as narration more than speech.

### 5. Backgrounds support the story and sit back

- Avoid empty panels and cluttered ones: "find that sweet spot right in the middle."
- A background "needs to scream how they feel": warm, airy and gently graded for calm; dark,
  heavy and desaturated for tension. Cool desaturated tones push things back; warm saturated
  ones pull the eye.
- Depth in three zones: a large, high-contrast foreground to frame the shot; the characters in
  the midground; a soft, pale, low-contrast background.
- Reuse is the norm: build a place once, save camera positions, switch between wide and close
  framings without redrawing the set.

### 6. Manga symbols carry what a simple face cannot

Emanata, the marks around a head, are part of the visual language the cast's style comes from:
the sweat drop (embarrassment, exasperation, dismay, shock; size and count give degree), the
anger vein (irritation; oversized, it tips a scene to comedy), vertical gloom lines (despair,
horror), blush marks, speed lines (motion, or converging lines to focus the eye), sparkles.
Chibi proportions are themselves used to shift tone toward comedy or cuteness. The two best
sources found were a French national library guide and a university anime resource.

### 7. Adapting prose: give each thought a visible container

- Do not paste interior monologue into captions by default. Ask what job a thought does, then
  choose a form: an expression, an action, a prop, a detail of the setting, one caption, or
  silence. Captions that restate what the face already shows fail.
- Cut what only repeats information; keep what changes the story; show what action, expression,
  setting, objects, distance and silence can carry. A whole flashback is often one image.
- Rough caption budget from one (vendor) blog: one to three short captions per six-panel page.
  Treat as a guess.
- A useful check: read only the pictures. If the story still makes sense, the visuals do the work.

### 8. Static can work, if it is deliberate

Dinosaur Comics has reused the same six panels since 2003 and carries the strip on its writing;
xkcd's stick figures are a vehicle for ideas. The pattern across minimal-art webcomics is that
cheap art makes a steady schedule possible and the writing does the drawing's work. That is a
reason to keep our output cheap, and a warning: a dramatic book needs the pictures to act.

### 9. Staging ideas from visual novels, which also live on a few still sprites

Cropping height changes intimacy (knees shows more of the design; hips puts the speaker closer
to the viewer). Where and how high a figure stands says who is above whom. A sprite that never
moves makes expressions repetitive and leaves the text box as the only interest; small shifts,
swaps and zooms fix much of it without new art. Pose variants should change the silhouette
(leaning, arms crossed), not only the face. These are general lessons from the source and a
developer's notes, not tested here.
